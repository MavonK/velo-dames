How a breakaway works (and why it sometimes succeeds)

Every stage starts the same way: a group attacks from the front and the peloton lets them go. But why? And why do sprinter teams sometimes fail to catch them?

The Stage 15 finish in Milan is a perfect example. Fredrik Dversnes won from a breakaway that the entire sprint peloton failed to catch.

The peloton tolerates a breakaway because the sprint teams are confident they can chase it down in the final kilometres. The catch: it takes perfect organisation, timing, and a bit of luck. When even one team hesitates or misjudges the gap, the break survives.

The three grand tours: Giro, Tour, Vuelta. What's the difference?

Three weeks of racing, 21 stages, mountains, sprints, time trials. The Grand Tours are the biggest events in cycling. Here's how to tell them apart.

The Giro d'Italia (May), Tour de France (July), Vuelta a España (September). Winning all three in a career is called the Triple Crown. Only a handful of riders have ever done it.

Each race has its own character: the Giro is known for brutal climbs and unpredictable weather, the Tour for prestige and the biggest prize money, the Vuelta for chaotic racing in the late-season heat.

The jerseys explained: what each colour actually means

Pink, yellow, red, polka dots, green. Cycling has a lot of jerseys and each one means something different. Here's your complete guide.

The overall leader wears pink at the Giro (Maglia Rosa), yellow at the Tour (Maillot Jaune), red at the Vuelta (Maillot Rojo). These three colours (no coincidence) are exactly the Velo Dames brand colours.

There are also jerseys for the best climber (polka dot at the Tour), the best sprinter (green at the Tour), the best young rider (white), and mountain points (blue at the Giro).
